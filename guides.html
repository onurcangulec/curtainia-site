Guides

Everything worth knowing before you commission a pair.

Four short guides from the bench: how to take a measurement that holds up, how much cloth a window really wants, what lining does to the light, and how to look after the fabric once it hangs.

Measuring a window

Width and drop are the two numbers that decide whether a made-to-measure pair works. Use a steel tape rather than a fabric one, and measure twice.

Width

Measure the full length of the track or pole you want covered, end to end — not the window. If you want the curtains to clear the glass when they are open, which we usually recommend, the pole should run 15–25 cm past the frame on each side, and that extra length counts.

Drop

Measure from the top of the track, or from the bottom of the rings if you are using a pole, straight down to where the curtain should stop. The usual finishing points are the sill, just below the sill, or the floor. For a floor-length pair, stop 1–2 cm short so the hem does not drag.

Four things that catch people out

  • Measure at the real mounting height, not at the window opening.
  • If the wall is not level, measure in two or three places and send us the largest figure.
  • Send centimetres or inches — just tell us which. Width first, then drop.
  • Unsure about a number? Send the figure you have plus a photograph. We would rather talk it through than re-cut.

Fullness & headings

Two choices decide how a pair reads once it hangs: how much cloth goes across the width, and how the top attaches to the track.

Fullness

Fullness is the ratio of cloth width to track width. At 2× a 100-inch track takes 200 inches of fabric. It is the single biggest reason a made-to-measure pair looks right or looks thin.

  • 1.5× — light. A flatter, relaxed drape. Good for a bold pattern you want to stay readable.
  • 2× — standard. Full folds without bulk. Suits most rooms and most cloth.
  • 2.5× — full. Noticeably richer folds. Our usual recommendation for a plain drape.
  • 3× — luxury. The fullest look. Best kept to lighter cloth, or the stack at the side of the window gets wide.

Headings

We make eight. The bench lists them with the fullness we use for each. In short: pinch pleats are the everyday answer, ripple fold is the modern one, grommet and tab top are the casual ones, and a flat panel is for a sheer meant to read as a plane.

Lining & light control

How dark a room goes is decided by the cloth and the lining together, and by where the curtain is fitted.

  • Unlined. The cloth alone. Light comes through and the colour glows. Right for sheers and for a drape in a room that does not need to be dark.
  • Lined. Adds weight and body, improves the hang, protects the face fabric from the sun and cuts a good deal of the light.
  • Blackout-lined. A coated lining behind an ordinary cloth. The fabric itself stops the light.

One honest warning: no curtain makes a room pitch dark on its own. Light finds the gap between the curtain and the wall. If you need a properly dark bedroom, mount the track higher and wider than the window and overlap the pair in the middle — or use a blackout roller blind behind the curtain.

Fabric care

  • Dust before you clean. A vacuum on low with a soft brush, twice a year, does more than washing.
  • Dry clean unless we say otherwise. Most of our cloth prefers it, and the quote tells you which fabrics are washable.
  • Never tumble dry. It shrinks the drop, and the drop is the one measurement you cannot get back.
  • Rehang damp if you can. The weight of the cloth pulls the creases out better than an iron does.
  • Sun fades everything. A lining roughly doubles the life of a face fabric in a south-facing window.

Ask the atelier
Common questions

Five we answer most weeks.

How far past the window should the pole go?

About 15–25 cm each side if the wall allows it. That gives the curtain somewhere to stack when it is open, so you get the whole window rather than half of it.

Should the curtain touch the floor?

Stop 1–2 cm short for a pair you open and close every day. A curtain that drags will wear at the hem and collect everything on the floor.

Do I need a lining?

Not always. A lining adds weight, improves how the cloth hangs, protects the face fabric from the sun and cuts the light. A heavy drape in a north-facing room often needs none.

Can you match a colour I have?

Send a photograph and, if you can, post us a small piece. We will send back the two or three cloths that come closest and let you judge them in your own light.

How do I wash them?

Most of our cloth prefers dry cleaning. Where a fabric is washable we say so on the quote. Either way: never tumble dry a made-to-measure curtain — it will shrink and the drop is the one thing you cannot get back.
